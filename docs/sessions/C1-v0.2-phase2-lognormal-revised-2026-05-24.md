# Phase 2 lognormal wording — reviewer-acceptable revision

**Goal.** Restate the v0.2 finding — that for S&P 500 the raw-tail Vuong test favors lognormal (R = −6.12) — in language that a PRE / Chaos / Physica A referee can accept, while keeping the original conclusion (the SOC verdict for this system rests on exponent-band agreement, not on rejecting the lognormal). The revision (i) is explicit about the statistical content of R = −6.12, (ii) labels the arxiv-02 prose error precisely as a *sign-interpretation* error rather than a numerical error, and (iii) gives a defensible scientific reason why exponent-band agreement is the appropriate decisive criterion for an SOC universality-class claim — not a fall-back after a failed test.

Status: this wording is a Claude-drafted reviewer pre-empt. **A domain-expert (econophysicist / statistical physicist familiar with the Clauset–Shalizi–Newman likelihood-ratio framework) should sign off before submission**, in particular on the procedural-tension framing in the second paragraph of §6.1.

## Replacement for §3.2, "Lognormal comparison — direction stated correctly" paragraph

(≈ 200 words — replaces the existing paragraph of the same name in
`C1-unified-preprint-draft-v0.2.md`.)

**Lognormal comparison — direction stated correctly.** Table 1 of the arxiv-02 source paper reports, for the power-law-vs-lognormal likelihood-ratio test on these data, the normalized log-likelihood ratio **R = −6.12** with significance **p = 9.3 × 10⁻¹⁰**. We adopt the Clauset–Shalizi–Newman 2009 convention (their Eq. C.5 and §C; see also Vuong 1989) in which the test statistic R is the sum of pointwise log-likelihood differences and is **positive when the power-law is favored and negative when the alternative is favored**; under that convention an R that is many standard deviations below zero with a small two-sided p-value is a *statistically distinguishable* preference *for the alternative*. The arxiv-02 abstract and §3.1 prose ("the power-law strongly dominating lognormal, p < 10⁻⁹") is therefore not a numerical mistake — the underlying R = −6.12 is correctly reported in its Table 1 — but a *sign-interpretation error*: the small p-value is read as evidence for the power-law without inspecting which direction R has fallen in. The corrected reading is that for unsigned S&P 500 daily returns the raw-tail Vuong test **favors lognormal over the power-law at the |R| = 6.12 level** (≈ 6σ on the standardized test statistic). The thirteen-system sibling manuscript reports R = −6.12 with the same sign convention and reads it correctly. We therefore restate the corrected direction here. The SOC verdict for S&P 500 (Section 4) does not rest on rejecting the lognormal; it rests on **exponent-band agreement** — the measured tail exponent α = 2.998 ± 0.041 lands inside one analytic standard error of the canonical inverse-cubic value α = 3.0 [Gopikrishnan et al. 1998] — and on the joint signature (power-law functional form, Omori decay at the daily band, null controls passing). The orthogonal raw-tail vs lognormal test is reported as a real qualification, not hidden. The power-law-vs-exponential test for the same fit is inconclusive (R = −0.52, p = 0.60), consistent with the limited discriminating power of likelihood-ratio tests at n_tail ≈ 2,300 over a narrow dynamic range [Clauset et al. 2009, §6.3]. The general implication of this finding — that a raw-tail lognormal comparison can favor lognormal without falsifying SOC membership of a system — is discussed in §6.1.

## Replacement for §6.1, "Lognormal is not always rejected — and is favored for S&P 500"

(≈ 280 words — replaces §6.1 of the existing draft.)

**6.1 Lognormal is not always rejected — and is favored for S&P 500.** The hardest alternative to a power-law tail is a lognormal, which can mimic power-law behavior over a finite dynamic range and which over n_tail ≲ 10⁴ samples is often statistically indistinguishable from a power-law by likelihood-ratio testing alone [Clauset, Shalizi & Newman 2009, §6.3]. We do not claim to reject lognormal on the raw tail of every system; for S&P 500 daily returns it is in fact favored at R = −6.12, p = 9.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ (§3.2).

We flag two procedural points the referee should weigh before drawing a conclusion from that single number. *First*, the Vuong-style normalized log-likelihood ratio is dominated by the small number of largest events in the upper tail, where a lognormal distribution naturally has more probability mass than a fitted power-law — so an R that favors lognormal on a Vuong test can coexist with a histogram or log-binned model selection that prefers the power-law (or power-law-with-cutoff). The thirteen-system sibling manuscript reports that on a complementary log-binned Bayesian-information-criterion test, power-law-with-cutoff is preferred over lognormal for every system tested, including S&P 500. *Second*, in the SOC universality-class framework the empirically decisive criterion is not "is the raw tail more likely under a power-law than under a lognormal at n_tail = 2,327," but "does the system reproduce the predicted critical exponent of its class, satisfy the predicted paired-signature structure (size power-law plus Omori temporal decay), and pass synthetic null controls applied through the same pipeline?" S&P 500 passes all three: the measured α = 2.998 lands on the canonical Gopikrishnan inverse-cubic value of 3.0 to within one analytic standard error; Omori decay at the daily band gives p = 0.286 ± 0.034 inside the published band [Weber et al. 2007]; and the same pipeline correctly rejects power-law on all four synthetic non-SOC nulls (Phase 5). The defensible framing, which we adopt here, is that the SOC verdict rests on this joint signature, with the raw-tail lognormal result reported as a real qualification — not the test that the SOC claim is staked on. A domain reviewer disagreeing with this framing has two well-defined options: (a) treat S&P 500 as a counter-example to inverse-cubic SOC and downgrade Phase 2's verdict in Table 1, or (b) reproduce the log-binned BIC test of the sibling manuscript on these data and report it alongside R = −6.12. We do not pre-empt that editorial choice; we surface the qualification clearly and recommend the reviewer pick one of those two paths if not satisfied with the joint-signature framing.

## Notes for the human reviewer

- **What this revision preserves from v0.2.** The numerical claim (R = −6.12, p = 9.3 × 10⁻¹⁰, lognormal-favoring) is unchanged. The labelling of the arxiv-02 prose error is unchanged (it is the sign that is read wrong, not the number). The SOC verdict for Phase 2 is unchanged.
- **What this revision tightens.** §3.2 now states the Clauset convention explicitly with citation to Eq. C.5 / Vuong 1989, removing any ambiguity about what "negative R favors the second model" means in this context. §6.1 now reframes the qualification in terms of *which criterion is decisive for an SOC class claim*, with a defensible reason (joint-signature criterion vs single-test criterion). This is the framing PRE / Chaos / Physica A referees in econophysics will expect.
- **What this revision does not settle.** Whether to also issue a published correction note for the standalone arxiv-02 paper. That is an editorial decision (carried over from C1 v0.2 checklist item 4) and is outside the scope of C1's preamble. A correction note is recommended on the merits — the sign-interpretation error is on the abstract — but is not in scope for the C1 preprint itself.
- **Why this is a "reviewer pre-empt" not a finished revision.** A working econophysicist may push back on the §6.1 framing on the grounds that an inverse-cubic claim for stock returns has historically been adjudicated against lognormal precisely by Vuong-style tests (Gopikrishnan et al., Plerou et al., Gabaix et al.). The defensible counter is that those papers used n_tail ≫ 10⁴ on individual-stock or tick-by-tick data where the Vuong test has good power, whereas the present n_tail = 2,327 of daily index returns is in Clauset's "limited-power" regime. A domain-expert sign-off should confirm that this is a correct reading of the relevant literature before submission.
